## Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi

*Kind:* individual. *Region:* Iraq. *Dates:* c. 780 – c. 850 CE (approximate). *Disciplines:* Mathematics, Algebra, Astronomy, Geography.

### Claims ledger
- **K-01 [DISPUTED]** His origin is uncertain: the epithet al-Khwarizmi suggests Khwarizm (south of the Aral Sea), Ibn al-Nadim says he came from there, but al-Tabari also calls him al-Qutrubbulli (a district near Baghdad); DSB suggests his ancestors, not he, may have come from Khwarizm.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: origin and epithets; BEA-K entry: origins. *Note:* Both sources say the evidence is mixed.
- **K-02 [DISPUTED]** His religion is read differently: DSB takes al-Tabari's epithet al-Majusi to mean at most Zoroastrian forebears (the pious preface of the Algebra shows an orthodox Muslim), while BEA reads the epithet as indicating a Zoroastrian rather than a Muslim.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: epithet al-Majusi; BEA-K entry: origins. *Note:* The two sources differ.
- **K-03 [VERIFIED]** He worked in Baghdad in close connection with the Abbasid court, especially under Caliph al-Ma'mun (reigned 813–833).  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: House of Wisdom and al-Ma'mun; BEA-K entry: opening paragraph. *Note:* Both read sources agree.
- **K-04 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** DSB says he became a member of the House of Wisdom under al-Ma'mun; BEA reports that Ibn al-Nadim attaches him to it but notes considerable modern controversy over what the institution was (library, or translation and research institution).  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: House of Wisdom and al-Ma'mun; BEA-K entry: opening paragraph. *Note:* Membership rests on a medieval report; the institution's nature is disputed.
- **K-05 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His dates are approximate: BEA gives born c. 780 and died c. 850; DSB gives born before 800 and died after 847.  
  *Sources:* BEA-K entry: dates; DSB-K entry: dates. *Note:* The two are compatible but differ in precision. No exact dates are known.
- **K-06 [DISPUTED]** Reports that he went on a mission to the Khazars, or took part in a geodetic expedition, are disputed: DSB thinks the Khazar and Seven Sleepers envoy was probably the Banu Musa's Muhammad; BEA says he may have joined expeditions but doubts the first has been raised (King 2000).  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: Khazar mission; BEA-K entry: expeditions. *Note:* Possible confusion with a Banu Musa brother (see B-01).
- **K-07 [VERIFIED]** He dedicated his Algebra and his astronomical work (the zij) to al-Ma'mun.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: dedications; BEA-K entry: works.
- **K-08 [VERIFIED]** Surviving and lost works: the Algebra survives in Arabic; the Hindu-numerals arithmetic is lost in Arabic but survives in Latin; the original zij is lost in Arabic and survives only in Latin versions of later revisions; the Geography and a Jewish-calendar treatise survive in Arabic; a Chronicle is lost but quoted by historians.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: works; BEA-K entry: works. *Note:* Both read sources agree on survival status.
- **K-09 [VERIFIED]** The Algebra, al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa'l-muqabala ('The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing'), treats equations of the first and second degree (reduced to six standard forms by 'completion' and 'balancing'), then mensuration, commercial problems and inheritance problems.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: the Algebra; BEA-K entry: works. *Note:* Described as practical mathematics, not an abstract treatise.
- **K-10 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** The Algebra uses no symbols (even for numerals): everything is written out in words, with shay' ('thing'), mal (square) and jidhr (root).  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: the Algebra. *Note:* Single source among those read.
- **K-11 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His Algebra is the first known (reported) work on algebra written in Arabic. This does not make it the first algebra anywhere: DSB stresses that Greek and Hindu algebra had already advanced further.  
  *Sources:* BEA-K entry: works; DSB-K entry: sources of the Algebra. *Note:* DSB says 'we are told' it was the first in Arabic: a report, not a proven fact.
- **K-12 [DISPUTED]** Where his algebraic methods came from is disputed: arguments for Hindu sources (rhetorical style, rule of three, circle formulas) and for Greek geometry (Euclid II); a closely related Hebrew treatise, Mishnat ha-Middot, is dated either c. 150 CE (Gandz) or to an early Islamic period (Sarfatti), so the direction of borrowing is unsettled.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: sources of the Algebra. *Note:* Reported as an open scholarly dispute.
- **K-13 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His treatise on Hindu numerals (Arabic title uncertain) expounds the digits 1–9, zero and place value; DSB thinks it likely that the decimal place-value system was a recent arrival from India and that his work was the first to expound it systematically, noting the Latin text is probably much altered.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: Hindu numerals. *Note:* Hedged in the source ('likely enough'). Not a claim that he invented the numerals.
- **K-14 [VERIFIED]** The Algebra was translated into Latin by at least two translators and strongly influenced elementary algebra in Arabic, Persian, Ottoman Turkish, Latin and European vernaculars.  
  *Sources:* BEA-K entry: works; DSB-K entry: influence and transmission.
- **K-15 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** DSB names Robert of Chester and Gerard of Cremona as the 12th-century Latin translators of the algebraic part; it notes the attribution to Gerard is complicated by a free adaptation also ascribed to him. The Latin Hindu-numerals text (early 12th century) led to 'algorismus' and the word 'algorithm'.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: influence and transmission. *Note:* Single source.
- **K-16 [VERIFIED]** His zij is a revision in the Zij al-Sindhind tradition, mixing Indian (Hindu) parameters and methods, Sasanian elements and Ptolemaic material.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: the zij; BEA-K entry: the zij. *Note:* Specifics differ in emphasis between the sources.
- **K-17 [DISPUTED]** The sources disagree on observations in the zij: DSB finds no trace of original observation in the extant tables (while noting the lost original introduction reportedly discussed Baghdad observations under al-Ma'mun giving an obliquity of 23°33′), whereas BEA says the tables include values determined by observations during al-Ma'mun's reign.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: the zij; BEA-K entry: the zij. *Note:* Preserved as a disagreement.
- **K-18 [VERIFIED]** No Arabic copy of his zij survives; it is known through Latin translation (probably by Adelard of Bath, early 12th century) of the revision by Maslama al-Majriti (d. 1007–08), and through the Toledan Tables. Later astronomers including al-Farghani and al-Biruni commented on it.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: the zij; BEA-K entry: the zij. *Note:* Commentators noted for relationships in Sub-batch 2B/2C.
- **K-19 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His Geography, Kitab surat al-ard, is mostly lists of coordinates arranged by climate zone; it is related to Ptolemy's Geography but not a translation of it, and DSB reports it is more accurate in areas ruled by Islam (notably a shorter Mediterranean) and introduces errors of its own. A derivation from a map, possibly al-Ma'mun's, is a scholar's conjecture.  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: the Geography; BEA-K entry: works. *Note:* DSB adds that it was unknown in Europe until the late 19th century.
- **K-20 [VERIFIED]** His Jewish-calendar treatise dates to no earlier than 823/824 (it carries an example for that year).  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: dating of works; BEA-K entry: works.
- **K-21 [DISPUTED]** His astrolabe writings survive only fragmentarily and scholars differ on whether the fragments are genuine; DSB thinks the content was probably drawn from earlier works.  
  *Sources:* BEA-K entry: works; DSB-K entry: astrolabe works.
- **K-22 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** DSB's judgement is that his scientific achievements were modest but extremely influential, because they transmitted Indian and Greek knowledge at a favorable time; BEA stresses the substantial influence of the Algebra. Other scholars' rankings differ (see lead L-MT, not read).  
  *Sources:* DSB-K entry: assessment; BEA-K entry: works. *Note:* An evaluation by the entry's author, recorded as such.

### Contributions (draft classification)

- **Algebra treatise: systematic reduction to standard forms**: mathematical_development, attribution confidence high. *Before:* Greek and Hindu algebraic knowledge, which had advanced further in places but did not resemble this presentation (DSB). *Contribution:* A practical, systematic handbook that fixed the terminology and form later Arabic algebra and Latin translations used. Not the first algebra, and the source of its methods is disputed (K-12).
- **Exposition of the Hindu numerals and place value**: commentary_or_synthesis, attribution confidence medium. *Before:* The Hindu numerals and decimal place value reached Baghdad from India; alphabetic and sexagesimal systems were in use (DSB). *Contribution:* A systematic exposition for Arabic readers. DSB hedges ('likely enough'); the Arabic original is lost and the Latin text may be altered. Not a claim that he invented the numerals.
- **Zij: revision of the Sindhind astronomical tradition**: commentary_or_synthesis, attribution confidence medium. *Before:* Earlier Zij al-Sindhind handbooks (BEA names al-Fazari), the Zij al-Shah and Ptolemy's tables (DSB, BEA). *Contribution:* A revision for al-Ma'mun that spread Indian astronomical methods and, via Latin, reached Europe. Whether it contains original observations is disputed (K-17).
- **Geography: coordinate tables of the known world**: commentary_or_synthesis, attribution confidence medium. *Before:* Ptolemy's Geography and probably al-Ma'mun's world map (a conjecture, DSB). *Contribution:* A more accurate map than Ptolemy's for Islamic lands according to DSB; unknown in Europe until the 19th century.

## Banu Musa (the sons of Musa ibn Shakir)

*Kind:* group. *Region:* Iraq. *Dates:* 9th century. *Disciplines:* Astronomy, Mathematics, Geometry, Mechanical inventions.

### Claims ledger
- **B-01 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** The Banu Musa were three sons of Musa ibn Shakir: (Jafar) Muhammad (died January or February 873), Abu al-Qasim Ahmad and al-Hasan (both died in the 9th century); all born in Baghdad at the start of the 9th century. BEA says separate biographies are quite impossible.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: header and opening. *Note:* One source. Not to be confused with al-Khwarizmi, whom some reports call 'Muhammad ibn Musa the astronomer' (see K-06).
- **B-02 [UNDER REVIEW]** Their father Musa ibn Shakir is described as a reformed bandit who became an astronomer or astrologer and a friend of al-Ma'mun; after his death al-Ma'mun became guardian of the brothers and sent them to the House of Wisdom.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: background. *Note:* BEA says 'is described as': biographical tradition, unchecked.
- **B-03 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** They worked with al-Ma'mun and his successors on geodetic surveys and on contracting public works, became wealthy, and used part of their wealth to sponsor scientific research and translation.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: background.
- **B-04 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** They obtained classical manuscripts from Byzantium by sending agents or travelling; on one trip Muhammad met Thabit ibn Qurra of Harran and brought him to Baghdad, where he joined their circle; Hunayn ibn Ishaq is described as part of their team.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: background. *Note:* Relationships with Thabit and Hunayn to be recorded in Sub-batch 2B. *Needs:* Second source.
- **B-05 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** About 20 books are known under the Banu Musa on astronomy, mechanics and mathematics; the majority of the astronomical works are lost.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: background; BEA-BM entry: astronomy. *Note:* Count as stated in BEA-BM.
- **B-06 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Muhammad's works include On the Visibility of the Crescent, a Book on the Beginning of the World, and a book variously titled on the motion of the celestial spheres / astronomy, which criticises the Ptolemaic system by explaining daily motion through rotation of all spheres and denying a ninth sphere.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: astronomy.
- **B-07 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Ahmad is reportedly the author of a geometric proof that there is no ninth sphere, texts on questions discussed with Sanad ibn 'Ali, a zij mentioned by Ibn Yunus (who also mentions a zij by the three brothers), and a Reasoning on the Trisection of an Angle.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: astronomy; BEA-BM entry: mathematics. *Note:* BEA words these as 'reportedly'.
- **B-08 [ATTRIBUTION UNCERTAIN]** The Book on the Solar Year was traditionally attributed to Thabit ibn Qurra, but recent research cited by BEA indicates it is most likely a misattribution and the work of the Banu Musa.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: astronomy. *Note:* Attribution moved by later research; one source.
- **B-09 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Their Book on the Measurement of Plane and Spherical Figures was recast by Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (13th century) and translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona (12th century) as Liber trium fratrum de geometria and Verba filiorum Moysi filii Sekir; BEA says its contents appear in works by Thabit ibn Qurra, Ibn al-Haytham, Fibonacci, Jordanus de Nemore and Roger Bacon.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: mathematics. *Note:* Influence claims are BEA's, not independently checked.
- **B-10 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Other geometry works attributed to them include three books related to Apollonius' Conics, a book on a proposition proved by Galen, and a Book on an Oblong Round Figure, which concerns the ellipse and describes the string-and-pegs ('gardener's') construction.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: mathematics.
- **B-11 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** BEA says they produced major work in mechanics that marks significant advances over Philo of Byzantium and Hero of Alexandria, whose works Muslim engineers knew well; its reference list cites the Kitab al-Hiyal (ed. al-Hassan, Aleppo 1981) and Hill's 1979 translation.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: mechanics. *Note:* BEA does not describe individual devices here. Device-level claims need Hill (L-HILL).
- **B-12 [UNVERIFIED]** Popular pages claim about 100 devices, early gas masks, a 'first programmable machine', a date around 850 and a division of labor between the brothers (Ahmad in engineering) that BEA does not give (BEA assigns astronomy and geometry works to Ahmad).  
  *Sources:* no source attached. *Note:* Not used as evidence. Verify against Hill's translation. *Needs:* Hill (1979) and al-Hassan (1981).
- **B-13 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Muhammad's son Nu'aym ibn Muhammad ibn Musa wrote a Book on Geometric Propositions, continuing the family tradition.  
  *Sources:* BEA-BM entry: closing.
- **M-B1 [MODERN SCIENTIFIC ASSESSMENT]** Modern astronomy does not use physical celestial spheres; the Banu Musa critique concerns the internal structure of the Ptolemaic cosmos and is a historical argument.  
  *Sources:* no source attached. *Note:* Drafted by ISA; no citation yet. *Needs:* Cite a history-of-astronomy reference.

## Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi

*Kind:* individual. *Region:* Iraq. *Dates:* c. 800 – c. 870 CE (approximate). *Disciplines:* Philosophy, Optics, Mathematics, Astronomy, Pharmacology.

### Claims ledger
- **Q-01 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi belonged to the Arab tribe of Kinda (hence 'philosopher of the Arabs'); he was born in Basra and educated in Baghdad.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.1. *Note:* Single source.
- **Q-02 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His dates are approximate: he died after 866 CE, usually put in the early 870s; his birth date is harder to pin down and is usually reckoned as around 800, inferred from service under al-Ma'mun (reign ended 833) and association with al-Mu'tasim (reigned 833–842).  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.1. *Note:* Inference, not a recorded date.
- **Q-03 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His career peaked under al-Mu'tasim, to whom he dedicated On First Philosophy and whose son Ahmad he tutored.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.1.
- **Q-04 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** He oversaw one of the two main groups of translators of the 9th century (the other led by Hunayn ibn Ishaq): the 'Kindi circle' rendered Greek philosophy and science into Arabic. He seems to have mediated between patrons and the translators, many of them Syrian Christians.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.1; SEP-K §6. *Note:* The circle produced the Theology of Aristotle (SEP §2.1). Relationship to Hunayn for Sub-batch 2B.
- **Q-05 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist (10th century) lists works ascribed to him, hundreds of treatises, with scientific and mathematical titles outnumbering philosophical ones; many are lost, and a single manuscript held in Istanbul preserves most of his extant philosophical writings.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.2. *Note:* No shelfmark is given in SEP-K, so none is recorded.
- **Q-06 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** On First Philosophy survives incomplete (first part, four sections); it argues the world is not eternal and God is a simple 'true One'. Its argument against eternity follows John Philoponus; SEP judges the argument weak and its success unclear.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.2; SEP-K §3.3. *Note:* A synthesis of Greek arguments, not an independent invention.
- **Q-07 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** On the Intellect survives in Arabic (few copies) and Latin and is the first treatise in the Arabic tradition to give a taxonomy of types of intellect.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.2; SEP-K §4.2.
- **Q-08 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His optics follows Euclid and Ptolemy (vision by rays emitted from the eye along straight lines) and criticises shortcomings in Euclid's optics; On Perspectives survives only in Latin. SEP says aspects of his account anticipate Ibn al-Haytham, who some decades later was first to explain vision accurately.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §5.1; SEP-K §1.2. *Note:* Not an anticipation of modern optics: see M-Q1.
- **Q-09 [DISPUTED]** On Rays (De radiis) survives only in Latin and its authenticity is disputed: a strong case against has been made (Matton 2022), while SEP says it may be partly based on authentic material.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §5.1. *Note:* Do not credit al-Kindi with its theory without further evidence.
- **Q-10 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** He wrote many works on astrology, treating it as a rational science with a physical theory of astral causation, and cosmological treatises (e.g. On the Prostration of the Outermost Sphere); his astrology was the most influential part of his work for Latin authors.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §5.2; SEP-K §6. *Note:* See M-Q2 for the modern assessment.
- **Q-11 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His extant scientific corpus is sizable (drugs, music, astrology, mathematics); he applied mathematics, especially geometry, across fields, and optics and medicine are well represented.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.2; SEP-K §5.1.
- **Q-12 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** His thought draws on Aristotle (main authority), Neoplatonism (Proclus, Plotinus), Philoponus, and Greek mathematical authors such as Euclid and Nicomachus.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §2.1.
- **Q-13 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** Al-'Amiri was a second-generation student of his through Abu Zayd al-Balkhi; Isaac Israeli and Miskawayh were influenced by him; later Arabic authors rarely cite him after the 10th century, while Latin authors valued his astrology and some translated works.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §6. *Note:* Persons not yet in the archive.
- **Q-14 [ATTRIBUTION UNCERTAIN]** On Definitions, ascribed to him, has doubted authenticity; it is almost certainly at least a product of his circle, composed in several strata.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §2.2.
- **Q-15 [ACADEMICALLY SUPPORTED]** SEP calls him the first self-identified philosopher in the Arabic tradition. This is the entry author's characterisation.  
  *Sources:* SEP-K §1.1. *Note:* Evaluative; not independently verified.
- **M-Q1 [MODERN SCIENTIFIC ASSESSMENT]** Modern optics explains vision by light entering the eye, not by rays emitted from the eye; the emission model was superseded.  
  *Sources:* no source attached. *Note:* Drafted by ISA from standard knowledge; no citation yet. *Needs:* Cite an optics or history-of-science reference.
- **M-Q2 [MODERN SCIENTIFIC ASSESSMENT]** Astrology has no empirical support in modern science; ISA records it as part of the historical natural philosophy of the period.  
  *Sources:* no source attached. *Note:* Drafted by ISA; no citation yet. *Needs:* Cite a history-of-science or astronomy reference.

- **On First Philosophy: Greek arguments for a created world and a simple God**: commentary_or_synthesis, attribution confidence high. *Before:* Aristotle, Neoplatonic texts and Philoponus' arguments against eternity (SEP). *Contribution:* Presented as Arabic philosophy's first systematic metaphysics in SEP's characterisation (Q-15), but its argument for a beginning follows Philoponus.
